The two systems don’t even start the week on the same day (Monday in the ISO Gregorian grid, Sunday in the Cotsworth/Eastman proposal), and after the first month the dates drift apart: every fixed month lasts exactly four weeks, with one day outside the week at the end of the year (and another mid-year in leap years).

Other calendars

The same day, counted in three more ways.

French Republican
Positivist (Comte)
Julian day

The Republican calendar was in official use from 1793 to 1805; here it is extended with its original rule (the year begins in Paris on the day of the autumn equinox). Auguste Comte’s Positivist calendar (1849) has 13 months of 28 days named after great figures of history and is an ancestor of the Fixed International calendar. The Julian day number (JDN) counts days continuously from 4713 BCE, with no months or years.

This day in deep time

Human-era note for the month

The Holocene calendar adds 10,000 years to the current year, counting from roughly the start of agriculture and settled life. Public holidays and the Christian movable feasts are calculated for the year shown; the pagan festivals and the saints follow Italian popular tradition. The deep-time section is a curated selection, not a complete list: for many ancient events the date is traditional or calculated, and some days have no event recorded (the nearest one is shown instead). Sunrise and sunset are an approximate astronomical calculation (about ±2 minutes).
